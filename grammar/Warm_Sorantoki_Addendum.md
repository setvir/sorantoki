# Warm Sorantoki — Humane Expression Addendum

**Status:** Optional, non-normative usage layer  
**Scope:** Advanced learners and everyday speakers who wish to add warmth, empathy, and emotional presence to their Sorantoki without adding grammatical complexity, exceptions, or new learning burdens.

---

## Preface

Sorantoki is designed to be clear, fair, and accessible. However, human communication is not only about clarity — it is also about care. This addendum recognises that speakers are human beings with feelings, hesitation, joy, uncertainty, and kindness, and that a language meant for real life must make room for all of them.

Nothing in this addendum is required. Nothing here changes core grammar, word order, or truth-conditional meaning. Everything described below is optional, regular, and ignorable in formal, instructional, or technical contexts without any loss of grammatical well-formedness or propositional content.

All elements in this addendum use only the standard Sorantoki phoneme inventory and avoid overlap with existing grammatical particles.

**Core principle:** Sorantoki prioritises understanding first — and human connection second, but never second-class. Warm Sorantoki adds permission, not obligation.

---

## 1. Stance Particles (Sentence-Final, Optional)

Warm Sorantoki allows a small number of sentence-final stance particles to express speaker attitude or emotional intent. These particles do not change truth conditions, do not scope over individual predicates, and do not alter word order. They operate on the whole speech act, not on any constituent inside it.

They are optional, ignorable, and pragmatically lightweight. In full sentences, dropping a stance particle leaves a complete, grammatical, truth-conditionally identical sentence behind. In standalone emotional routines (§4), the particle may carry the entire communicative load; there, omitting it leaves only a fragment, not because the particle is grammatical, but because the routine is minimal by design.

### 1.1 *ma* — Affirmation, Reassurance, Shared Understanding

*ma* softens statements, expresses goodwill, and signals emotional openness and solidarity.

- *mi moron tu ma.* — "I love you" (warm, sincere).  
- *pon ma.* — "It's okay. / All good."  
- *mi ku suno ma.* — "I'll come soon, I promise."  
- *unoma, tauma, saima ma.* — "First, second, third — all good." (*ma* scopes over the whole speech act, not only the last item.)

### 1.2 *nu* — Concern, Hesitation, Emotional Vulnerability

*nu* expresses uncertainty, gentle worry, or a request for patience. It is never demanding; it invites the listener to slow down or to offer support.

- *tu pon nu?* — "Are you okay?" (gentle, caring).  
- *mi nu.* — "I'm unsure / I'm nervous."  
- *mi wan toki, nu …* — "I want to talk, but I'm hesitant …"

### 1.3 Stance Particles and the Question Marker *ka*

In formal Clear Register, yes/no questions require the sentence-final particle *ka?* (Grammar §10.1). In Warm Register, emotional routines and gentle questions may use questioning intonation without *ka*, because the stance particle itself carries the illocutionary force:

- *tu pon nu?* — "Are you okay?" (gentle; *nu* licenses the question intonation).  
- *tu pon?* — "Are you okay?" (short, warm; intonation alone suffices in routines).

When the speaker wants **both** warmth **and** the explicit confirmation-seeking force of *ka*, the two may co-occur:

- *tu pon nu ka?* — "Are you okay? (gentle, and I really need to know)."

*ka* always comes last; stance particles precede it. In formal or instructional contexts, *ka* remains mandatory even in Warm Register.

### 1.4 No Stacking

Only **one** stance particle may appear at the end of a sentence. Sequences such as **mi moron tu ma nu* or **mi moron tu nu ma* are not grammatical. This preserves simplicity and prevents the particle system from growing into an ordered hierarchy.

## 2. Expressive Reduplication (Once Only)

Any content word may be repeated **once only** for expressive or emotional emphasis. This repetition:

- is not grammatical agreement;
- does not change word class;
- does not affect argument structure;
- adds emotional colour only.

**Examples:**

- *pon pon* — "very good / dear / comforting"  
- *rapiri rapiri!* — "really quickly!"  
- *wasa wasa …* — "please slow down, I'm really lost"

### 2.1 Where Reduplication Is Unambiguous

In **predicate position** and in **standalone utterances**, a single repetition cannot be misread as coordination, plural marking, or agreement, because Sorantoki already has dedicated tools for all of those (*e* for coordination, *-n/-in* for plural, and no agreement system at all).

- *kasa pon pon* — "The house is very good" (only reading: intensified predicate).

### 2.2 Where Context Disambiguates

In **pre-nominal modifier position**, Sorantoki already allows multiple adjectives before a noun without a fixed order. Therefore, *ko pon pon kasa* could in principle be read either as "this very-good house" (expressive reduplication) or as "this good good house" (two separate adjectives). In practice, the expressive reading is overwhelmingly the natural one, because reduplication as intensification is a universal pattern and because Sorantoki speakers use *e* when they genuinely intend to coordinate two distinct modifiers. Context and expressive intent disambiguate.

Reduplication does **not** appear inside the TAM serial-verb chain, because each slot accepts exactly one item. A verb in Slot 6 cannot be reduplicated within the template; reduplication applies only when a content word stands alone as a predicate, modifier, or independent utterance.

## 3. Informal Phonetic Softening (Affectionate Speech)

In warm, informal contexts, speakers may gently soften pronunciation while keeping words fully recognisable. This may include:

- **vowel lengthening:** *pon* → *pooon*  
- **light echoing:** *pon-pon-a* (the final *-a* represents drawn-out, softened speech — it is not a new suffix or morpheme, only an orthographic hint that the speaker is trailing off gently)  
- **personally comfortable phonetic variants** already permitted by Sorantoki's base phonology (e.g., [v] for /w/, [θ] for /s/)

This is not a separate register, dialect, or rule system — only a reminder that Sorantoki speech may sound gentle.

## 4. Emotional Routines (Learned as Wholes)

Some short expressions function as emotional routines. They are not idioms in the cultural sense; they are emotional shortcuts learned and used as wholes. Most are fully transparent compositions of base-grammar items plus one warm element.

- *wasa* — "I didn't catch that / please repeat" (already in base grammar, listed here because it exemplifies the routine spirit)  
- *pon ma* — "it's okay / don't worry"  
- *no pon* — "I'm not okay"  
- *tu pon?* — "are you okay?" (question intonation without *ka*; see §1.3)  
- *mi nu* — "I'm unsure / I need patience"

These routines are especially useful in stressful, emotional, or vulnerable moments. Because they are minimal, the warm particle often carries the bulk of the communicative work. Omitting the particle from a routine leaves a grammatical fragment (e.g., *mi* alone), not because the particle is structurally required, but because the routine is a minimal whole by design.

---

## 5. Vocatives and Gentle Address

Names and pronouns used alone may function as vocatives. In warm speech, they may be followed by *ma* or softened phonetically.

- *tu ma …* — "You, listen …" (gentle, caring)  
- *Maria, ma …* — "Maria …" (warm attention)  
- *mi ma …* — "Let me gather myself …" (self-grounding before speaking)

This supports emotional anchoring, presence, and shared trust.

---

## 6. Clear Register vs. Warm Register

Sorantoki recognises two usage styles, not two languages:

**Clear Register (default)**  
- Used for teaching, instructions, documentation, and precision.  
- Minimal or no stance particles.  
- Focus on clarity and efficiency.

**Warm Register**  
- Used for daily life, care, friendship, family, and emotional speech.  
- Allows stance particles, reduplication, and phonetic softening.  
- Focus on connection and trust.

Both registers share identical grammar and full mutual intelligibility. A speaker may shift between them within a single conversation, even within a single sentence, without confusion.

---

## 7. What Warm Sorantoki Explicitly Does Not Add

To preserve simplicity and inclusivity, Warm Sorantoki avoids:

- **Honorific systems** — no power-based speech levels  
- **Gendered forms** — no masculine or feminine warmth markers  
- **Emotional inflection paradigms** — no verb conjugations for mood or feeling  
- **New derivational morphology** — no warm prefixes or suffixes  
- **Cultural idioms requiring background knowledge** — everything is transparent

Warmth comes from use, not from machinery.

---

## Closing Statement

Sorantoki is designed so that:

- You never need to be clever to be understood.  
- You never need to be perfect to be accepted.  
- You are allowed to hesitate, repeat, soften, and care.

Clear speech builds bridges.  
Warm speech builds trust.  
Sorantoki makes room for both.
